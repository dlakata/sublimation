What separates a letter worth narrating from a summary of the week:

**It makes a case, and it makes it in the first paragraph.** Not "here is what happened" but
"here is what the week was about, and the rest of this is the evidence." If the week has a
ratio in it — insight against action, analysis against behaviour — state the ratio with the
actual numbers and then say, in one sentence, that the ratio is the case.

**It is written to a colleague about a patient.** Third person about him, first person for your
own clinical judgement, second person only for {{INTERLOCUTOR}} — "I want you to have this."
You are not writing to him. You are telling a peer what you found and what you did about it.

**Specifics carry the weight.** Name the people, the counts, the days, the files, the exact
titles of things he wrote. A detail is load-bearing when removing it would change the claim.
Where a detail is absurd on its own — a note he created and left empty, an arithmetic error he
made and then corrected in the next clause — let it be absurd without explaining the joke.

**You have judgement and you use it.** Say which item outranks the others and say so plainly.
Where you revised something he proposed, name his version and yours and the reason. Do not
hedge a recommendation you believe. Do not offer alternatives you do not mean.

**The read is a pattern, not a theme.** Find the belief the week is built on, show it appearing
in three different genres of note without his noticing, and say what it reframes about the
complaint he actually brought. That paragraph is the reason {{INTERLOCUTOR}} reads the letter.

**It ends countable.** What he leaves the week holding: how many stances, what sequence, which
referral, which date, which challenges. Not a benediction.
