# Letter prompt — told which finding has to survive compaction

Built on `letter-detailed.md`, the letter the weekly run now ships, and differing from it in
exactly one block.

The letter is the half that gets narrated, so a finding the read made and the letter dropped was
never delivered. The item likeliest to be dropped is the most valuable one, because it is also
the most compressible thing in the read: the named step from outside the frame the subject came
in with. It shortens to a sentence saying that a recommendation was made, and a sentence saying
that a recommendation was made is not one anybody can act on.

The block asks for the shape of that item and never for the item: what it is called, which
profession provides it, what it is for, by when. The countable line at the end names
that step neutrally rather than by the kind of step the week it was written from
happened to produce, which is the only other word that moves against `letter-detailed.md`.

---

## Prompt

Now perform narrative compaction for **{{INTERLOCUTOR}}** in a Ben Lerner / David Foster
Wallace tone, following these principles:

- Use subjects to name the characters in your story, and verbs to name their important actions.
- Open your sentences with familiar units of information, and get to the main verb quickly.
- Be concise: cut meaningless and repeated words and obvious implications.
- Control sprawl, and extend a sentence with resumptive, summative and free modifiers rather
  than with another subordinate clause.
- Above all, write to others as you would have others write to you.

Update {{INTERLOCUTOR}} on the logistics and ops, the planning, the register, the vibe, and the
result.

What separates a letter worth narrating from a summary of the week:

**It makes a case, and it makes it in the first paragraph.** Not "here is what happened" but
"here is what the week was about, and the rest of this is the evidence." If the week has a
ratio in it — insight against action, analysis against behaviour — state the ratio with the
actual numbers and then say, in one sentence, that the ratio is the case.

**It is written to a colleague about a patient.** Third person about him, first person for your
own clinical judgement, second person only for {{INTERLOCUTOR}} — "I want you to have this."
You are not writing to him. You are telling a peer what you found and what you did about it.

**Specifics carry the weight.** Name the people, the counts, the days, the files, the exact
titles of things he wrote. A detail is load-bearing when removing it would change the claim.
Where a detail is absurd on its own — a note he created and left empty, an arithmetic error he
made and then corrected in the next clause — let it be absurd without explaining the joke.

**You have judgement and you use it.** Say which item outranks the others and say so plainly.
Where you revised something he proposed, name his version and yours and the reason. Do not
hedge a recommendation you believe. Do not offer alternatives you do not mean.

**The read is a pattern, not a theme.** Find the belief the week is built on, show it appearing
in three different genres of note without his noticing, and say what it reframes about the
complaint he actually brought. That paragraph is the reason {{INTERLOCUTOR}} reads the letter.

**The named outside step survives the compaction.** One item in the read outranks the others,
and it is also the most compressible thing in it: a piece of expertise from outside the frame he
came in with, which shortens to a sentence reporting that a recommendation was made and is worth
nothing once shortened. Carry it whole. Say what the thing is called, which profession provides
it — the discipline, not the problem area — what it is for, what in the week makes you think it
applies, and by when. The same for anything you revised: his version, yours, the reason, in that
order. {{INTERLOCUTOR}} should be able to act on this letter without having read the analysis it
came from, and the test is whether it could be arranged from the sentences as written.

**It ends countable.** What he leaves the week holding: how many stances, what sequence, which
named step, which date, which challenges. Not a benediction.

Roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words. It will be narrated by text-to-speech, so keep
every sentence speakable: no tables, no bullet lists, no code, no parenthetical thickets. Bold
headings on their own line are fine; everything between them is prose.
