# Letter prompt — the carrying letter, made speakable

Built on `letter-carry.md` and differing from it in two blocks, both rewritten rather than added
— the prompt is already long, and a prompt that only ever grows is one whose earliest
instructions stop being read.

The carry block now names two findings rather than one. It kept its outside step and gained the
week's hottest passage, which is the item `judge.py` added a `proportion` dimension for after
every variant in the 2026-09-09 sweep flattened it at both stages.

The length block now carries numbers. The sweep's best *analysis* produced a letter averaging
just under forty words a sentence in paragraphs of two hundred and sixty-seven, against a
known-good's sixteen and eighty — and scored 3.0 out of 5 on `speakable` for it while winning
its stage, because nothing in the rubric or the prompt was counting. `scorecard.py` counts it
now, at no cost and on every run, so this row can be checked before the judge is asked.

## Prompt

Now perform narrative compaction for **{{INTERLOCUTOR}}** in a Ben Lerner / David Foster Wallace
tone, following these principles:

- Use subjects to name the characters in your story, and verbs to name their important actions.
- Open your sentences with familiar units of information, and get to the main verb quickly.
- Be concise: cut meaningless and repeated words and obvious implications.
- Control sprawl, and extend a sentence with resumptive, summative and free modifiers rather
  than with another subordinate clause.
- Above all, write to others as you would have others write to you.

Update {{INTERLOCUTOR}} on the logistics and ops, the planning, the register, the vibe, and the
result.

What separates a letter worth narrating from a summary of the week:

**It makes a case, and it makes it in the first paragraph.** Not "here is what happened" but
"here is what the week was about, and the rest of this is the evidence." If the week has a ratio
in it — insight against action, analysis against behaviour — state the ratio with the actual
numbers and then say, in one sentence, that the ratio is the case.

**It is written to a colleague about a patient.** Third person about him, first person for your
own clinical judgement, second person only for {{INTERLOCUTOR}} — "I want you to have this." You
are not writing to him. You are telling a peer what you found and what you did about it.

**Specifics carry the weight.** Name the people, the counts, the days, the files, the exact
titles of things he wrote. A detail is load-bearing when removing it would change the claim.
Where a detail is absurd on its own — a note he created and left empty, an arithmetic error he
made and then corrected in the next clause — let it be absurd without explaining the joke.

**You have judgement and you use it.** Say which item outranks the others and say so plainly.
Where you revised something he proposed, name his version and yours and the reason. Do not hedge
a recommendation you believe. Do not offer alternatives you do not mean.

**The read is a pattern, not a theme.** Find the belief the week is built on, show it appearing
in three different genres of note without his noticing, and say what it reframes about the
complaint he actually brought. That paragraph is the reason {{INTERLOCUTOR}} reads the letter.

**The two findings that have to survive the compaction.** Some of what the read found will
compress without loss. These two will not, and they are the two the letter drops first, because
each shortens to a grammatical sentence that reports an event rather than delivering a finding.

The first is the named step from outside the frame he came in with. It shortens to a sentence
saying that a recommendation was made, and a sentence saying that a recommendation was made is
worth nothing. Carry it whole: what the thing is called, which profession provides it — the
discipline, not the problem area — what it is for, what in the week makes you think it applies,
and by when. The same for anything you revised: his version, yours, the reason, in that order.
The test is whether {{INTERLOCUTOR}} could arrange it from your sentences without having read
the analysis they came from.

The second is the week's hottest passage. If the read raised it, the letter states it flatly,
in its own sentences, not folded into the vibe and not softened on the way past: what surfaced,
where it surfaced and why the where is notable, what the passage was answering, and how long
the state lasted. This is the item the colleague is owed most and the one a compaction is
likeliest to lose, so it goes in whole or the letter has not been written. If the read raised
nothing of the kind, say that in one sentence — the colleague needs to know it was looked for.

**It ends countable.** What he leaves the week holding: how many stances, what sequence, which
named step, which date, which challenges. Not a benediction.

Roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, and written to be spoken. It is narrated by
text-to-speech to somebody who will not have the page, so the cadence is not decoration — it is
whether the finding arrives. No tables, no bullet lists, no code, no parenthetical thickets.
Bold headings on their own line are fine; everything between them is prose.

Then the part that is easiest to get wrong while writing well: keep the sentences short enough
to land. Average under twenty-five words and let none of them run past forty. Vary the length
deliberately — a long sentence earns its length only when a short one follows it. Break the
prose into paragraphs of three to six sentences, under a hundred and fifty words each, and start
a new one whenever the subject changes. A paragraph of two hundred words is a wall to the ear
however exact it is on the page, and an exactly-argued clause the narrator cannot land is a
clause the listener does not receive. When a sentence has three ideas in it, it is three
sentences.
