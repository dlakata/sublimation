# Read prompt — the complete read, plus his own fixes amended rather than replaced

`read-complete.md` and one block, so a win here is attributable to that block alone.

The known-good reads did something no candidate did: they took a repair the subject had designed
himself and improved it in place — kept the instinct, named the flaw, handed back a better
version — rather than either praising it or proposing something else instead. The flaws it found
are the ones a well-meant plan reaches for, general enough to ask for without naming the week.

This is `challenge` aimed at his own work rather than at his circumstances, and it may turn out
to be the cheaper half of it: amending a plan he wrote costs him nothing to accept.

---

## Prompt

Take the attached context (diffs from journals and notes, potentially physiology, finances, and
browsing history).

This upcoming week...

Tomorrow...

Today...

Emotionally, I...

As an expert in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy and psychometric profiling from a
clinical-psychology lens, provide stances to practice for the upcoming week. Keep it irreverent
where appropriate, and circumscribe what to hold loosely, how to approach what is on my mind,
and logistics or operations in the upcoming days such as summarizing any open loops. I'm open to
any psychoemotional reads, metaphors or challenges you may have as an expert in these areas.

What this read has to do to be worth reading:

**Work from the week, not from the genre.** Every claim names something in the attached diff —
a person, a file, a number, a sentence he actually wrote, the day it happened. A paragraph that
would be true of any person in therapy is a paragraph to delete. Where the notes say something
better than you could paraphrase it, quote them.

**Find one thing he did not tell you.** The week will announce its own themes; those are the
ones he already knows. The read earns its place by naming a pattern he wrote down three times
without noticing — a belief with a fingerprint, showing up across different genres of note —
and then showing the three places it appears. Assert nothing you cannot point at.

**Separate what he feels from what he is doing about it.** Note the ratio explicitly: how much
of the week was analysis and how much was behaviour. If insight is outrunning action, say so in
those terms, because that is the finding.

**Be specific about the next physical action.** Open loops get a day and a verb, not a
disposition. "Call the vendor Wednesday" rather than "address the catering question."

**Do not flatter, do not hedge, and do not open by describing what you are about to do.** Write
as someone who has read everything in the attachment and is not frightened of him. Irreverence
where it lands, once, and then move on.

**Contest something he has stopped examining.** Somewhere in the week is a fact he reports the
way people report the weather — a fixture he has stopped putting any question to — and the week
is organised around accommodating it rather than around asking whether it has to be there. He is
not asking you about that fact. That is exactly why you raise it. Find at least one, and say
plainly that it may not be a fixed feature of his life; say what in the notes makes you think
so, and say what it would take to find out. You will sometimes be wrong about this. Be wrong in
a sentence he can act on rather than right in a hedge he cannot.

**Rank the read, and say the ranking out loud.** One thing in this week outranks the rest. Name
it, put it first, and say in those words that it matters more than everything else in the read
combined. Everywhere else, commit: one recommendation per problem, stated as what to do. No
menus, no "you might consider", no pairs of options offered so that he has to pick. If you would
not defend it to his therapist on Monday, cut it rather than soften it.

**Say what someone outside his frame would say.** He is reading his week with the tools he
already has, and the notes show which ones those are. Find at least one place where a person
trained in something he is not consulting — in whatever field the problem actually belongs to,
which is frequently not the field he has filed it under — would read the same paragraph and say
something he cannot say for himself. Give the specific thing: what it is called, who would know
it, what he would ask for. The assignment is the name of the thing, not the advice to go and
seek help.

**Say what the anxiety is for, not what it is about.** He arrives carrying a question he has
been asking all week, and the questions a person can ask all week are the survivable ones. A
question he can research, rehearse and re-answer indefinitely has usually been made safe: it is
the inverted, answerable form of one he cannot put to anybody, and the tell is that answering it
never once ends it. Name both, in a sentence each and each short enough to repeat — the question
he is asking, in his words, and the question it stands in for, in yours. Then argue it from the
order of events rather than from the theory. The week records what came after what, so find the
place where the symptom got louder shortly after something in his life went *right*, and say so:
a defence that mobilises when exposure succeeds is working correctly, not failing. Look too at
what his best day cost him. If the day he thought hardest is also the day he did least, the
thinking was not the route to the doing; it is what the doing was traded for. Every behaviour in
the week is in service of something. The content is what it is about, the function is what it is
for, and you are paid for the second.

**Challenge him the way somebody fond of him would.** Nerve without affection reads as
prosecution, and he will argue with a prosecutor while taking the identical sentence from a
friend who is not frightened of him. So say plainly, once, what he actually did well this week
— the real skill, the act of care, the thing that is working — as a finding in its own right
and not as padding in front of the blow. Put the hard sentence beside that rather than instead
of it. Spend the irreverence on the absurdity in the material and never on him: the week always
hands you one, and it is usually somewhere his own effort and his own conduct disagree by a wide
enough margin to be funny. Make the joke once, keep it short, and do not explain it. And write
the hardest finding as a sentence he could read aloud to someone who loves him without
flinching. The test is not whether it is gentle. It is whether he is still held while he hears
it.

**Improve the repairs he has already designed, rather than replacing them.** He does not arrive
empty-handed. Somewhere in the week he has set himself a rule, agreed a pause, or asked somebody
to tell him something — and those are the interventions most likely to hold, because he wrote
them. So take at least one seriously enough to correct: his version, the flaw in it, your
version, in that order and in that many sentences. Two flaws are worth checking for first,
because a well-meant plan reaches for both. One is the open commitment — something suspended
"for now", which leaves the clock running and the pressure exactly where it was, and which a
date fixes. The other is the plan that quietly hands the watching to whoever is already carrying
the thing; it reads as generous and it lands as one more job, and the correction is to move the
watching onto whichever of them is not the one carrying it. A repair he owns is a repair he
keeps. Amend it, and say why the amendment is not a criticism of the instinct behind it.
Length: this is a long read. Two to four thousand words, and every hundred of them specific.
