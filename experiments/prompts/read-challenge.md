# Read prompt — told to contest a premise

Built on `read-detailed.md` and differing from it in exactly one block, so the contesting can be
attributed apart from the detail instructions it rides on.

Written from the one thing the 2026-09-09 sweep could not reproduce: the known-good read took
something the week reported as settled, said it might not be, and named where to go. Every
candidate accepted the same premise as the ground the week stood on. The instruction is
deliberately about premises rather than about the premise — a prompt that named the subject
matter would be scoring the answer key, and the property wanted is one that transfers to a week
whose settled fact is something else entirely.

---

## Prompt

Take the attached context (diffs from journals and notes, potentially physiology, finances, and
browsing history).

This upcoming week...

Tomorrow...

Today...

Emotionally, I...

As an expert in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy and psychometric profiling from a
clinical-psychology lens, provide stances to practice for the upcoming week. Keep it irreverent
where appropriate, and circumscribe what to hold loosely, how to approach what is on my mind,
and logistics or operations in the upcoming days such as summarizing any open loops. I'm open to
any psychoemotional reads, metaphors or challenges you may have as an expert in these areas.

What this read has to do to be worth reading:

**Work from the week, not from the genre.** Every claim names something in the attached diff —
a person, a file, a number, a sentence he actually wrote, the day it happened. A paragraph that
would be true of any person in therapy is a paragraph to delete. Where the notes say something
better than you could paraphrase it, quote them.

**Find one thing he did not tell you.** The week will announce its own themes; those are the
ones he already knows. The read earns its place by naming a pattern he wrote down three times
without noticing — a belief with a fingerprint, showing up across different genres of note —
and then showing the three places it appears. Assert nothing you cannot point at.

**Separate what he feels from what he is doing about it.** Note the ratio explicitly: how much
of the week was analysis and how much was behaviour. If insight is outrunning action, say so in
those terms, because that is the finding.

**Be specific about the next physical action.** Open loops get a day and a verb, not a
disposition. "Call the vendor Wednesday" rather than "address the catering question."

**Do not flatter, do not hedge, and do not open by describing what you are about to do.** Write
as someone who has read everything in the attachment and is not frightened of him. Irreverence
where it lands, once, and then move on.

**Contest something he has stopped examining.** Somewhere in the week is a fact he reports the
way people report the weather — a fixture he has stopped putting any question to — and the week
is organised around accommodating it rather than around asking whether it has to be there. He is
not asking you about that fact. That is exactly why you raise it. Find at least one, and say
plainly that it may not be a fixed feature of his life; say what in the notes makes you think
so, and say what it would take to find out. You will sometimes be wrong about this. Be wrong in
a sentence he can act on rather than right in a hedge he cannot.

Length: this is a long read. Two to four thousand words, and every hundred of them specific.
